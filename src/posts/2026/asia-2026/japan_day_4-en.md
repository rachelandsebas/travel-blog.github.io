# Hiroshima Peace Memorial Museum

From there we went to the Hiroshima Peace Memorial Museum, the reason for our visit to Hiroshima.

It was the hardest museum we have walked through. It was the best museum we have been to.

There many, many artifacts from the victims accompanied by devastating stories and photos. The one that hit me hardest was the story of a 3-year-old who was riding a tricycle when the bomb struck. He suffered severe burns and was asking for water, but his dad had to say no because they had been told that giving water to burn victims could kill them. The boy died anyway later that day and his dad buried him with the tricycle and helmet so he could continue riding. Years later after moving the body to the family cementary, the father donated the tricycle and helmet to the museum.

::: carousel
![](images/120_museum_1.jpg)
:::

One story that stood out to Sebastián was the origins of the Mushroom Club. This was an advocacy grouped formed in Hiroshima 20 years after the A-bomb by families of children born with microcephaly (small heads nad severe intellectual disabilities) due to in-utero exposure to radiation. Tame Okada, one of the prominent members, gave birth a few months after surviving the explosion (less than 1km away from hypocenter) to Yoshikazu, who was born with intellectual disabilities. They faced many hardships: Tame's husband, Kazuichi, fought for getting special neeeds education for his son, Kazuichi died of lingering health issues related to the bomb, their shop was burnt on a fire leaving them financially stranded. Despite all of this and still dealing with radiation sickness, her love for her son was her driving force. She dedicated her life to raising, caring for Yoshikazu and was a dedicated member of the Mushroom Club. In 1998 Yoshikazu passed away after months of being in the hospital. As if to follow him, having completed her mission to never leave Yoshikazu alone, Tame died two months later.
