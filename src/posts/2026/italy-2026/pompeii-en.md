# The ancient city

The ancient city of Pompeii was buried under 4-6 meters of ash in 79 AD when the nearby volcano, Mt Vesuvius, erupted. The ash protected and preserved the city for more than 1500 years, until it began being uncovered by archeologists.

Currently, about two thirds of the city have been excavated and preserved. The city gives us a great picture of daily life in Rome.

Much of the biological material (people, animals, wood, plants), was either carbonized immediately or has decomposed over the years, but the ash hardened around them first, so they leave empty pockets in the ash. Archeologists use the pockets like moulds, filling them with plaster. These plaster casts allow us to see the shapes of objects and people in their final moments.

The stone walls, roads, and objects in the city were extremely well preserved (although most of the roofs collapsed under the weight of the ash during the eruption).

The first building we entered, the gymnasium (school), has been turned into a museum and holds many artifacts. The rest of the city stands as is. We were impressed with the size of the city and how much you could just wander wherever you wanted.

# Around the city

There were so many buildings!! A few that stand out were:

Restaurants: Many non-wealthy people didn't have a kitchen in their home, meaning they would buy all their meals from restaurants (Sebastian is jealous). There were so many restaurants around the city. They could be identified by counters with holes where the jars of food were inserted.

Shops: There were lots of shops along the main roads. They can be identified by their wide doorways. Most used to be two stories (with the owners living above), but the second floors collapsed.

Homes: The wealthy houses had many rooms surrounding a central atrium/garden. The main rooms have beautiful intricate frescos, often depicting scenes from myths.

Bakeries: The bakeries have big ovens. Archeologists found bread still in some of the ovens.

Roads: (Not a building, but...) the roads we walked on are the original roads. Most had raised sidewalks on either side (since the road doubled as a sewer). Occasional stepping stones allowed you to cross without having to step in the road. You can still see the grooves worn in the stones from the carriages.
